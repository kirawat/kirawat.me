> "I choose a lazy person to do a hard job. Because a lazy person will find an easy way to do it".[^1]

While the sentiment has been popularly linked to Gate's supposed management philosophy, its actual origin is a subject of considerable debate and lacks direct evidence from the man himself.

The enduring connection of this quote to Bill Gates likely stems from its alignment with a certain perception of programmer and engineering culture—a culture that values elegant, simple, and efficient solutions to complex problems. In this context, "laziness" is not seen as a character flaw but rather as a driver for innovation and the avoidance of unnecessary effort.

The core idea behind the quote, however, predates its association with Bill Gates by several decades. The concept of a "lazy" but ingenious individual finding the most efficient solution has been expressed by others in the realms of engineering and management.

One of the earliest similar sentiments can be traced to [Frank B. Gilbreth Sr.](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/gilbreth-frank-and-lillian), a pioneer in time and motion studies in the early 20th century. Gilbreth, known for his work on workplace efficiency, is said to have advocated for finding the "one best way" to perform any task and reportedly saw the value in a "lazy" worker's inclination to find shortcuts:[^2]

> Gilbreth made sure his employees knew the "one best way" to carry out their jobs, but he was open to suggestions from them and looked for innovative ideas or products that would make their jobs easier. When he could not find something he needed for the business, he invented it himself.

Another notable precursor comes from Clarence Bleicher, an automobile executive who, in a 1947 testimony before a US Senate committee, is recorded as saying something to the effect of putting a "lazy man" on a difficult job to find a simpler way to accomplish it. Here is his quote, excerpt from a passage printed in the "Omaha World Herald" of Nebraska:[^3][^4]

> When I have a tough job in the plant and can't find an easy way to do it," Mr. Bleicher said, "I have a lazy man put on it. He'll find an easy way to do it in 10 days. Then we adopt that method."

Many innovations are born from a natural human "laziness"—a fundamental desire to increase efficiency, reduce effort, and improve our overall quality of life.

"What is the easiest way to do this?" This single question has been the catalyst for countless inventions, from the remote control that saves us a walk across the room to the automation software that handles our most tedious tasks. By constantly seeking the path of least resistance, we are driven to create solutions that not only boost efficiency but fundamentally improve our life by freeing up our time and energy for more meaningful pursuits.
