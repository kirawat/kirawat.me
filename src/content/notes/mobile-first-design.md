A mobile-first stragtegy encourages a design process that focuses on the core user experience from the outset. By designing for the constrainsts of a smaller screen first, you ensure that the most critical content and features are accessible to all users, regardless of their device.

## History

The concept of "mobile-first" is most famously credited to Luke Wroblewski, a prominent product director at Google and an influential figure in the design community.

He began articulating the idea around 2009 and 2010 through a series of blog posts (["Mobile First"](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?933), ["An Event Apart: Mobile First Responsive Design"](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?1846)) and in talks at conferences. His ideas were then consolidated and popularized in his book, ["Mobile First"](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?1389), published in October 2011.

While Wroblewski championed the "mobile-first" banner, the strategy itself stands on the shoulders of earlier web design principles like Progressive Enhancement, a concept introduced by developers like Steven Champeon and Nick Finck in the early 2000s. Progressive Enhancement advocates for starting with a baseline of content and functionality that works for all browsers and then adding more complex features for more capable browsers.

## How and Why It Started

The mobile-first strategy was born out of necessity, driven by several key factors:

1. **The Explosion of Mobile Browse:** The launch of the first iPhone in 2007 was the primary catalyst. For the first time, users had a powerful and user-friendly internet browser in their pockets. This led to a dramatic and rapid increase in mobile web traffic.

2. **The Failure of "Graceful Degradation":** The traditional web design workflow was "desktop-first". Designers would create a full-featured, complex website for a large desktop monitor and then attempt to shrink it down for mobile. This process, known as graceful degradation, was proving to be problematic. The results were often cluttered, slow, and frustrating user experiences on mobile devices because developers were trying to remove or hide elements from an already complex design.

3. **Recognizing Mobile Constraints as Strengths:** Wroblewski's core insight was to flip this process on its head. He argued that [designers should embrace the constraints of a mobile device](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?870) (smaller screen, slower connection, touch interface) as a starting point. This forces a focus on what is truly essential.

  * **Focus on Core Content and Functionality:** With limited space, you have no choice but to prioritize the most important content and user tasks. This leads to a cleaner, more focused product.

  * **Improved Performance:** Building for mobile first encourages leaner code and optimized assets from the beginning, resulting in faster load times.

  * **Innovation:** Designing for mobile encourages the use of device-specific capabilities like GPS, cameras, and touch gestures, leading to more innovative and context-aware applications.

4. **The Rise of Responsive Web Design:** Around the same time, Ethan Marcotte introduced the concept of Responsive Web Design (RWD) in a 2010 article on *A List Apart*. RWD provided the technical means (using fluid grids and media queries) to make a single website adapt to multiple screen sizes. The "mobile-first" philosophy provided the stretegic "why" and "how" for RWD, as its approach is the most logical and efficient way to implement a responsive design.
